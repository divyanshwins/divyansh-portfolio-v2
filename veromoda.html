Why Vero Moda was losing users before they even arrived

In brief

A graduation project inside Bestseller's e-commerce team. Paid campaigns were converting far below expectation and the working theory was targeting. Six hundred thousand users of GA4 data said otherwise: the product page took eight seconds to show anything, and the thing meant to catch the survivors — the recommendation engine — was a preset list being shuffled. Three interventions came out of it, and two of them moved numbers I can show.

01 — Background

The company, and the business problem

Bestseller is a Danish fashion group behind Vero Moda, Jack & Jones, Only and Selected Homme, operating in India through Bestseller India. Its online growth runs on performance marketing — campaigns that send a user from an ad straight onto a product page, skipping the homepage entirely. That model has a consequence most fashion retailers underrate: page speed is not an engineering metric, it is the first thing the media budget buys.

The brief arrived as a marketing problem. Spend was up, conversion was not, and the assumption in the room was that campaigns were reaching the wrong people.

01Spend was not converting. Performance marketing budget was growing without a matching return across the paid product-page campaigns.
02Paid users left immediately. Sessions arriving from ads exited before any meaningful interaction — no scroll, no gallery, no size selection.
03The hypothesis worth testing. If users leave before engaging rather than after, the failure is not who was targeted. It is what they landed on.

02 — Where the users go

Where do users drop?

634,884 new users and 713,306 engaged sessions were pulled out of GA4 and split by acquisition channel first. Two things stood out immediately: nearly two thirds of traffic was arriving unassigned — GA4 could not attribute it to any channel at all — and email was contributing 0.19% of new users, which for a fashion retailer with a customer database is not a small number, it is an absent one.

How might we find out what happens in the seconds between a paid click and an abandoned product page — a window GA4 records as a session with nothing in it?

03 — User flow investigation

Two dominant entry journeys

Flow analysis showed almost no traffic entering through the homepage. Campaigns were dropping users directly into product surfaces, and two patterns accounted for nearly all of it — both ending the same way.

Flow 01 — straight to the product page

Ad click Product page Exit

Users landed on the product itself and left without scrolling or interacting. No dwell time was recorded — the session existed, and nothing happened inside it.

Flow 02 — into the listing page

Ad click Listing page Exit

Users who landed on a listing left without finding anything relevant. Weak recommendation and filtering surfaces gave them no second thing to look at.

Two flows, two different failures — and the distinction mattered, because they do not have the same fix:

AAd → PDP drop. Slow product-page loading causing abandonment before engagement. A speed problem — sections 04 and 05.
BAd → PLP drop. Ineffective product discovery due to poor recommendation and filtering. A relevance problem — section 06.

04 — Critical discovery

The bottleneck was the page itself

The largest exit cluster was not happening after users read something and decided against it. It was happening during page load, in a window where there was nothing on screen to decide about. That single observation moved the investigation off marketing and onto engineering.

Put every event on one time scale and the argument needs no commentary. The main thread is still blocked at nine seconds. The largest element does not arrive until eight. The window in which people give up opens long before either.

First paint lands at 3.6 seconds and the page is still not finished — the hero image, the thing a fashion customer actually came for, arrives at eight. In between, the main thread is locked, so the page does not merely look unfinished: it cannot respond to a tap either. Users were staring at a locked, partially rendered page and leaving before there was anything to judge.

06 — UX engineering

Interface decisions driving the bloat

A Lighthouse report tells you the page is heavy. It does not tell you who made it heavy. Inspecting the page as a designer rather than as an auditor found two interface decisions — both reasonable in isolation — that between them explained most of the weight.

Observation 01

Image navigation rendering

Every product page dynamically generated one DOM dot indicator per product image. A garment shot from twenty angles produced twenty dots, twenty click handlers and twenty layout recalculations — then did it again for the next product, and the next, across hundreds of products in a browsing session.

Before — one dot per image

20 nodes · 20 handlers · 20 recalculations, per product

After — in-frame arrows

2 nodes · 2 handlers · constant, regardless of image count

Performance impact

Excessive DOM nodes and JavaScript recalculation inflate Total Blocking Time — the exact metric that was already at 2,090 ms.

Recommended fix

Replace dot navigation with simple in-frame arrow controls. The node count stops scaling with the number of photographs.

Observation 02

High-resolution product images

Fashion e-commerce cannot compromise on image quality. Fabric texture, colour accuracy and garment detail are not decoration — they are the purchasing signal, and the reason a customer buys online at all. So images were being uploaded at maximum resolution with no format optimisation, accounting for over 2 MB of avoidable payload per page.

JPG / PNG, as uploaded ~800 KB
WebP, same dimensions ~300 KB
Business constraint

Reducing resolution was not feasible — visual clarity is a primary purchasing signal, and the merchandising team was right to defend it.

Recommended fix

Change the format, not the picture. WebP holds visual quality while cutting file size by roughly 60% — around 2 MB back per page, and nothing lost that a customer can see.

07 — The recommendation engine

The safety net was a shuffled list

Speed explains why people left the product page. It does not explain why the ones who stayed never went anywhere else. So the second half of the project went after the surface that is supposed to catch them: You may also like.

What was actually running turned out to be simpler than anyone assumed. Recommendations came from pre-defined lists, one per product category, populated with popular or high-margin items. To create the impression of variety, the same list was shuffled into a different order on every page. Every T-shirt page in the catalogue therefore showed the same recommended T-shirts — just in a different sequence each time.

01No personalisation. The lists were static and consulted no user data at all — not purchase history, not browsing behaviour, not demographics. One-size-fits-all, so the recommendations were generic by construction.
02No trend detection. Nothing in the system identified what was currently selling. In fast fashion, where the whole business model is turning trends into stock quickly, the recommendation surface was structurally behind the merchandising floor.
03The knock-on effect. Irrelevant suggestions do not just fail to convert — they teach the user that this surface is not worth looking at, which is how a page stops having a second act.

A recommendation is an intersection, not a list

I surveyed the standard approaches first — collaborative filtering, which finds users like you; content-based filtering, which finds products like the one you are looking at; and the hybrid of the two. Collaborative filtering finds patterns content-based filtering misses; content-based filtering still works for a brand-new product or a brand-new user, where collaborative filtering has nothing to go on. Fashion retail needs both, constantly.

The design decision was to stop treating a recommendation as a list to be filled and start treating it as a bucket left over after four filters. Each ring below removes candidates; what survives all four is what gets shown.

All products matching the user's preferences — the properties of what they bought or browsed The category they are navigating right now Filters the user has applied Top-converting products

The recommendation bucket — four filters, narrowing. Redrawn from figure 5.1 of the report.

The ordering is the argument. Preference comes first because it is the only ring that is about the person; commercial performance comes last, so “what sells” can only ever break a tie between things the user already plausibly wants — it never gets to override them. That is the line the old preset list crossed.

Four kinds of user, four strategies

The other half of the algorithm is that a shopper is not always the same shape. Identification happens first — via login, cookies or both — and the user falls into one of four states depending on what evidence exists about them. Each state gets its own strategy, because each has a different kind of signal available:

New userNo purchase, no wishlist

Nothing to go on but this session, so track the properties of what they are viewing right now — category, brand, colour, size, material, style, price band — and recommend against those. Demographics layer on top where available.

Only wishlisterWishlist, no purchase history

Prioritise the properties of wishlisted items, then use content-based filtering to add complementary pieces often bought alongside them — things that do not share the same properties but do share the same outfit.

Only purchasedPurchase history, no wishlist

Prioritise properties from what they have actually bought — the strongest signal there is — and extend it with frequently-bought-together items against those past purchases.

Purchased and wishlistedBoth signals present

Weighted. Purchased properties carry the highest weight because money is the firmest statement of preference; wishlist properties carry a lower weight, because a wishlist is where taste is still moving.

Matching is then two measures rather than one, because a garment has two kinds of property. Jaccard similarity handles the categorical ones — a floral, long-sleeved, midi dress shares three of three features with another, or one of three, and scores accordingly. Cosine similarity handles the numerical and the weighted ones, so a user who has clicked several red dresses and bought a bodycon gets red bodycon dresses ranked above merely floral ones. Jaccard says these are the same kind of thing; cosine says and this is how much you seem to like it.

It was written as a working Python prototype — pandas and scikit-learn over two files, user_behavior.csv and product_catalog.csv, producing a ranked list of product IDs with the user's own category prioritised and the remaining slots filled from the wider catalogue. Enough to demonstrate, not enough to deploy: the company took the approach forward through a vendor offering the same algorithm as an API, which was the faster route to production and the right call.

The surface it renders into had to change too. The existing three-column carousel was the wrong container on a phone — at mobile width barely one card was visible per row, and the horizontal scroll needed to reach the rest was an interaction users almost never performed. A better algorithm feeding a container nobody scrolls is still nothing.

Before3-col carousel

Only one product visible per row at mobile width. Everything else sits off-screen behind a horizontal swipe that most users never make.

After2-col vertical

Four products visible immediately, more arriving through progressive loading on the scroll gesture users already perform.

08 — Into the inbox

The same engine, inside the email

Email was contributing 0.19% of new users. The reflex answer is to send more email. The actual problem was that a Vero Moda email was a static picture with a link on it: every action a recipient might take required leaving the inbox, loading the eight-second page from section 04, and starting again.

So the recommendation engine was pointed at the inbox instead, using AMP for Email — which allows genuinely interactive components inside the message. The personalised bucket renders as a swipeable carousel in the email itself, with product detail, size and colour variants and add-to-cart available without a page load ever happening.

The recommendation arrives, rather than waiting

Instead of a generic campaign creative, each recipient's email is populated by the same four-filter bucket that runs on site. The relevance work is done once and reused in both places.

The click-through hurdle is removed

Browsing, choosing a size and adding to cart happen inside the message. The slowest, leakiest step in the journey — loading a product page — is skipped entirely for this audience.

Templates for every brand

Cross-sell, PDP-dropper, wishlist and cart templates, built out across Jack & Jones, Vero Moda, Only and Selected so one system covers the group rather than one brand.

Fallbacks are not optional

Not every client supports AMP. Every template ships with an HTML fallback, because a feature that silently fails for a third of your list is a worse outcome than the static email it replaced.

09 — Two interface interventions

Further down the funnel, two smaller fixes

The funnel in section 02 showed two more leaks worth attention: 76.8% abandoning between cart and checkout, and a returns problem that never shows up in a funnel at all. Both were A/B tested against a control.

Intervention 01

Say what the garment actually measures

Size-related returns are the structural tax of online clothing, and the cause is a size chart that says S, M, L when the shopper needs centimetres. Benchmarking against Snitch, Rare Rabbit and Blissclub showed the same gap everywhere. The intervention: the moment a size is selected, a pop-up shows that garment's real measurements — UK and US equivalents, bust, waist, hips, shoulder — so the decision is made against a body rather than against a letter.

What was measured

Demographics, time spent on product pages, number of size changes before settling on one, and returns attributed to size discrepancy — control group against treatment group.

Outcome

Higher confidence at the point of selection and fewer size-related returns in the treatment group. The cheapest kind of win: no new technology, just information moved to where the decision happens.

Intervention 02

Collapse the checkout

Traditional checkout asks for billing and shipping separately, then payment, then reveals the real total at the end. On a phone that is three screens of typing followed by a surprise. The redesign merged address selection and payment method into one step, added address autocomplete from existing user data, and kept the full price — taxes and shipping included — visible throughout rather than at the end.

Checkout completion rate, cart abandonment rate and time spent in checkout, A/B tested against the traditional flow.

Outcome

Bounce rate at the payment gateway fell by 40%. Fewer steps, less typing and no price surprise — the friction removed was not decorative, it was the whole of it.

11 — Takeaway

The finding was the deliverable

By mapping the drop-off to a specific performance bottleneck and a specific relevance failure rather than a marketing problem, the investigation reframed the solution entirely. The brief that arrived asked which audiences to stop paying for. The answer was that the audiences were fine — they were arriving, in their hundreds of thousands, at a page that could not show itself to them in time, and being offered a shuffled list if they waited.

The part I would carry to the next investigation is the order of operations. The channel table said which door, the funnel said where, the flow analysis said which journey, Lighthouse said why, and only reading the interface as a designer said who caused it — twenty dots, an unoptimised JPEG and a list nobody had looked at since it was written. None of those three appears in an analytics dashboard. Performance work stops at diagnosis unless somebody is willing to go and open the page.

Scope, honestly. This was my graduation project, carried out inside Bestseller's e-commerce team between January and April 2024, and submitted to NIFT. The analysis, the algorithm design, the interface work and the email system are mine; implementation on the live storefront was carried out by the third-party agency that maintains it, from the specification and code handed over. The movements in section 10 are the figures recorded in the report, and the Lighthouse diagnostics in sections 04 and 05 are from a separate audit of the live product page. Everything else — the funnel, the channel table, the algorithm, the A/B tests — is in the 70-page report, including the Python implementation and the full template set.
